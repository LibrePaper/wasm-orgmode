* Abstract

We compare three shrinkage estimators for small-area means when the
sampling variance is known only approximately. Under a normal hierarchical
model, the *empirical Bayes* estimator dominates the unpooled mean whenever
the between-area variance $\tau^2$ is not large relative to the sampling
variance. We report /simulation/ evidence and an application to county-level
unemployment, and we ship the code in =smallarea=.

* Introduction

Survey estimates for small areas are often too noisy to publish. A county
with forty respondents can report an unemployment rate that is off by
several points, and a policy reader cannot tell signal from sampling noise.
Borrowing strength across areas is the standard remedy, and the question
this paper answers is how much to borrow.

Our contribution is threefold. First, we give a closed form for the
shrinkage weight under approximate variances. Second, we show by simulation
that the weight is robust to a misspecified variance. Third, we apply the
estimator to the county panel described in [[file:data/county-panel.csv][the data appendix]], and we
document every step in the supplementary notes.

** Related work

The Fay-Herriot model [fn:fh] is the usual starting point. Earlier work
treats the variance as known; we relax that assumption and keep the
hierarchical structure.

* Model

Let $y_i$ be the direct estimate for area $i$, with sampling variance $v_i$.
The model is

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Areas are exchangeable a priori: knowing the value in one area tells us
something about the others, but not everything.
#+END_QUOTE

\begin{equation}
y_i \mid \theta_i \sim N(\theta_i, v_i), \qquad \theta_i \sim N(x_i^\top \beta, \tau^2).
\end{equation}

The shrinkage weight for area $i$ is

$$
B_i = \frac{v_i}{v_i + \tau^2}, \qquad \hat\theta_i = (1 - B_i)\, y_i + B_i\, x_i^\top \hat\beta.
$$

When $\tau^2$ is large the weight $B_i$ goes to zero and the estimator
reduces to the direct mean. When $\tau^2$ is small every area is pulled
toward the regression prediction \(x_i^\top \hat\beta\).

** Estimating the variance component

We estimate $\tau^2$ by restricted maximum likelihood. The estimate is
truncated at zero, which is the usual convention, and the standard error
comes from the observed information matrix. Table [[tab:sim]] summarises the
bias of the estimator over the simulation grid.

* Simulation study

We draw $m = 50$ areas with sample sizes between 10 and 200 and compare
four estimators: the direct mean, the Fay-Herriot empirical Bayes estimator,
a fully Bayesian version with a half-Cauchy prior, and a nonparametric
shrinkage rule.

#+NAME: tab:sim
#+CAPTION: Mean squared error relative to the direct estimator, by true $\tau$.
| Estimator         | tau = 0.5 | tau = 1.0 | tau = 2.0 |
|-------------------+-----------+-----------+-----------|
| Direct            | 1.00      | 1.00      | 1.00      |
| Empirical Bayes   | 0.41      | 0.63      | 0.92      |
| Half-Cauchy       | 0.44      | 0.65      | 0.94      |
| Nonparametric     | 0.47      | 0.61      | 0.90      |

The empirical Bayes estimator is never worse than the direct mean in these
runs, and it is best whenever $\tau$ is small. The half-Cauchy prior is a
little more conservative, which costs it efficiency when the truth is close
to the prior mode.

** Procedure

The steps of one replicate are:

1. draw the true area effects $\theta_i$ from the hierarchical model;
2. draw the direct estimates $y_i$ with the known variances $v_i$;
3. fit each of the four estimators on $(y, v, x)$;
4. record the squared error of each estimate against $\theta$.

Each configuration is repeated 2000 times, and the reported numbers are
averages over replicates. The sampling variances are perturbed by up to
twenty percent in a second run to check robustness.

* Application

The application uses unemployment rates for 312 counties. The direct
estimates come from the labour-force survey and the covariates come from
administrative records. The code for the fit is below.

#+BEGIN_SRC R :results output :exports both
library(smallarea)

fit <- fay_herriot(
  y = panel$rate,
  v = panel$variance,
  X = model.matrix(~ unemp_lag + log_pop, data = panel),
  method = "reml"
)

summary(fit)
#+END_SRC

The shrinkage is largest in the counties with the smallest samples, which
is the intended behaviour. The largest revision in the panel is about
1.8 percentage points, in a county with fewer than twenty respondents.

** Findings

- Shrinkage is strongest where the sample is smallest.
- The covariates explain about a third of the between-area variance.
- The estimates are stable under a twenty percent perturbation of $v_i$.

Ranking the counties by the shrunken estimate changes the top ten in only
two places, which matters for the programme that uses the list.

* Discussion

The main limitation is the normality assumption. When the area effects are
skewed, the weight $B_i$ is still sensible but the interval around
$\hat\theta_i$ can be too narrow. A nonparametric alternative is available,
and it performs about as well in the simulations, but it is harder to
explain to a non-specialist audience.

We also assume the covariates are measured without error. The application
uses lagged administrative counts, which are close to the target quantity
but not identical to it.

** Future work

The natural extension is to let the variance itself depend on area size,
which would remove the need to treat $v_i$ as known. We have a draft
implementation and will report it separately.

* Conclusion

Empirical Bayes shrinkage with an estimated variance component is a
reasonable default for small-area survey estimates. It is simple to fit,
it reduces error in every scenario we tried, and its behaviour is easy to
explain: the noisier the estimate, the more it is pulled toward the
prediction.
